Introduction

This is a quick guide for Deriv content writers.

Important things to remember:

        ●​ Write clearly and concisely. Always aim for accuracy.​

        ●​ Make it as easy as possible to understand. Use appropriate language for the target
           audience.​

        ●​ Deriv clients are global; many of them aren’t native English speakers. Content may be
           translated into other languages, so use simple language as much as possible.




        Deriv’s tone of voice
        ●​ Clear and direct
         ○​ Use straightforward, jargon-free language that is relatable. Ensure
            communications are easy to understand and convey core messages
            efficiently.
●​   Friendly and approachable
         ○​ Create a welcoming atmosphere through a warm, inviting tone that connects
            with the audience. Use language that fosters a sense of community and
            approachability.
         ○​ Incorporate supportive phrases that express empathy and readiness to assist,
            reinforcing the idea of being a helpful partner.
●​   Client-centred
         ○​ Centre all content around the needs and interests of the audience,
            emphasising how Deriv can enrich their experience.
         ○​ Communicate key information clearly, ensuring audiences feel supported and
            informed at every stage of their interaction with Deriv.
●​   Trustworthy and transparent
         ○​ Maintain honesty in all communications by being upfront about processes,
            potential risks, and any relevant guidelines. Strive for transparency to foster
            trust.
         ○​ Ensure consistent messaging across all platforms, reinforcing reliability and
            the Deriv brand identity.
●​   Informative and engaging
         ○​ Aim to educate and inform by sharing valuable insights that improve
            understanding and decision-making. Highlight relevant topics that cater to
            the audience's interests.
         ○​ Encourage interaction and engagement, inviting feedback and making
            communication feel more like a two-way conversation.
General guidelines

We use British English in all our content​
The only exception is when it comes to unique names. For example, the U.S. Department of
Labor and Microsoft Media Center.

Common spelling differences between British English and American English:

                      UK                                              US

                 Programme                                         Program

                    Centre                                          Center

                    Colour                                           Color

                   Cheque                                           Check

                   Analogue                                         Analog

                  Authorise                                       Authorize

                   Favourite                                       Favorite

                     Fulfil                                         Fulfill

                    Metre                                           Meter

        Licence (noun), license (verb)                     License (noun and verb)

       Practice (noun), practise (verb)                    Practice (noun and verb)


Proofread, proofread, proofread
Proofreading helps readers to focus on your message, not your mistakes.

Keep it simple and be clear
Use simple, conversational language. Use short, uncomplicated sentences and paragraphs.
Avoid jargon at all costs. If you must use a technical term, clearly define it so everyone can
understand.

Know our audience
We produce content that caters to people from around the world with various interests and
backgrounds. We should be inclusive and speak the language that our clients use. Write from
the perspective of the reader.

Avoid redundancy
Examples: New innovation, future plans, prior experience, throughout the month of June.

Avoid ambiguity
Be specific. For example, say, “We’ll get back to you in two days” instead of “We’ll get back
to you soon”.

Avoid using fillers
Avoid using words like “furthermore”, “however”, “therefore”, etc.

Write using active voice
Active voice is preferred because it makes our writing much easier to understand. It puts the
sentence's subject first and performs the action that the rest of the sentence describes.

Write positively
Use positive language rather than negative language. For example, “Volatility Indices are not
available in some countries” can be written as “Volatility Indices are available in selected
countries.” ​
​
Anglo-Saxon over Latinate (for English)​
Use simplified verbs such as “need/give” instead of “require/provide”.

Contact us
Clients can reach us via
    ●​ Live chat
    ●​ WhatsApp

Insert the above links in all client emails.
Grammar and punctuation

Headlines, headings, titles
Don’t use periods in headlines, headings, and titles.

If you’re writing an ad, periods can be considered if you’re aiming for strong, short
statements, clarity, and precision or to separate words distinctively (in lieu of commas).


   ●​ Write better. Faster. Together.
   ●​ Brilliant. In every way.

Periods, question marks, exclamation points
Periods go inside quotation marks. Question marks go inside quotation marks if they’re
part of the quote. Use exclamation points sparingly and never more than one at a time.

Don’t use a period for abbreviations written with the word's first and last letter.
   ●​   Saint: St
   ●​   Doctor: Dr
   ●​   Mister: Mr
   ●​   Feet: ft
   ●​   Limited: Ltd
   ●​   Professor: Prof
   ●​   Sergeant: Sgt
   ●​   Versus: vs

Put a space between numbers and units of measure.
    ●​ 200 cm
    ●​ 1 kg

Don’t use a period with abbreviations for units of measure in the metric system.
  ●​ Centimetre: cm
  ●​ Metre: m
  ●​ Kilogram: kg


Ampersand
Use only when part of a formal name or title.
   ●​ Ogilvy & Mather
   ●​ Barnes & Noble

For team or department names and other scenarios, use “and”
    instead.
   ●​ Accounts and Payments
   ●​ Terms and conditions
Comma
Always use a serial comma if you’re listing three or more items in a sentence.
   ●​ Cats, dogs, and elephants
   ●​ Cats, dogs, elephants, and giraffes

Use a comma before a conjunction when it joins two independent clauses.

   ●​ After you set a password, withdrawals will be paused for 24 hours, and you’ll be
      logged out of all sessions.
   ●​ Your account has been verified, and you can now start trading.

Don't use a comma between two imperative clauses.
   ●​ Save your changes and restart the system.
   ●​ Upload your ID and confirm your address.

Colon
A colon can be used to precede a list of items in a sentence, especially when you want
to direct attention to the list.
   ●​ In headings, always capitalise the first word after the colon.
   ●​ For a point on the list, after the list label, always capitalise the first word after
      the colon. Example:
          ○​ The “wow” moment: One of the speakers, Benny Leong, mentioned an AI
              tool that fixed an old photo in 30 seconds.
   ●​ If two independent clauses are joined with a colon to make a longer sentence,
      do not capitalise the first word after the colon. Example:
          ○​ Raunak was clear: it’s not a mindless usage of AI that will replace what
              we do.



Semicolon
Use a semicolon when multiple commas are being used in a series of items.
...Product Design and UX; Compliance and Risk Management; and the Accounts and
Payments teams.

Apostrophe
Use an apostrophe to contract two words.
   ●​ I’m
   ●​ We’re
   ●​ She’d

Don’t use an apostrophe to pluralise dates and acronyms.
  ●​ CFDs, not CFD’s
  ●​ 1950s, not 1950’s
Don’t use an apostrophe if it can be read as a compound noun with the same meaning.
Trade on world markets... (not Trade on the world’s markets...)

Single quotation marks
Use to indicate names and titles when necessary and to indicate a quote within a quote.
“The CEO specifically asked, ‘What’s Deriv Trader’s monthly trading volume for October?’”
said Dennis.

Double quotation marks
Use to indicate direct quotes. Never use it to emphasise a certain word or phrase.
Exceptions can be made for terms and conditions.

Ellipses
Use only to indicate omitted words or phrases in quotes. Use three dots in a row with no
spaces between each dot.
   ●​ If only she had... (Change the example)

Hyphen
Used to join words to indicate that they have a combined meaning or that they are
linked in the grammar of a sentence.


Numbers between twenty-one and ninety-nine should also be hyphenated when
they’re spelt out.
   ●​   Malaysia-Indonesia border
   ●​   First-time trader
   ●​   Ten-year-old technology
   ●​   To-do list
   ●​   Highly acclaimed, critically acclaimed, nationally known (don’t hyphenate if the
        first word is the adverb, ‘very’, or an adverb that ends in -ly)

En dash ​
Used between figures (like dates and times) to show a range.
   ●​ 1995–2001
   ●​ The shop is open 9:00 am–5:00 pm

Em dash
Em dashes set off extra information, mark a break in a sentence, or draw attention to a
specific point. It’s ok to use spaces on both sides of the em dash or use no spaces.
   ●​ The team, led by their captain—a seasoned veteran—advanced to the finals.
   ●​ The reason for her absence was clear—she was ill.
   ●​ The most important thing — the key to success — is perseverance.

Formulas
If you’re writing a formula that requires the multiplication sign, use the actual character
instead of a lowercase X. In Google Docs, you can do this by going to Insert > Special
characters. Use a space before and after a special character (addition, subtraction,
multiplication, and division) in the formula.

Capitalisation
We use the sentence case throughout our apps, websites, and any form of communication
with the outside world. Always capitalise the first word in a sentence and capitalise proper
nouns.

Page names in a navigation menu within a user interface, whether on web or mobile, should
use sentence case. For example:

   ●​ Go to your Profile settings to change your photo.
   ●​ See the current status of your transfer on the Transfer status page.

Exception: We use lowercase on the sign-up screen when the word "or" acts as a separator
between two sign-up or log-in options (e.g., "Sign up using A or B").




Book titles are put in the title case (all words are capitalised except for articles and
prepositions).

For legal documents, like terms and conditions, terms that are defined can be capitalised.

Payment agents

Capitalisation rules for “Payment agents” are as follows:

   ●​ Capitalised in:
         ○​ The terms & conditions (where it’s defined as a specific term)

   ●​ Sentence case (e.g., “Payment agents”) in all other contexts.

the standard currency rule) but "Wallet" always keeps its capital W, because it's a Deriv
product name, not a common noun.

(USD, EUR, GBP) — never partially lowercased.

Why this rule exists

Two capitalisation rules intersect here, and it's easy to accidentally apply the wrong one to
the whole phrase:

   1.​ Currency names are common nouns → lowercase ("US dollar," not "US Dollar").
   2.​ "Wallet" is a Deriv product name → always capitalised, even mid-sentence.

Writers sometimes capitalise the whole term as if it were one proper noun ("US Dollar
Wallet") or lowercase the whole thing as if it were one common phrase ("us dollar wallet").
Neither is right — each half follows its own rule independently.




Capitalisation after a colon​
The word after a colon is usually capitalised, whether what follows is a complete sentence or
not. For example:

   ●​ Reminder: Your feedback stays anonymous.
   ●​ Who will benefit: Employees, clients, partners

Exception: If what follows a colon is a parenthetical phrase/list that is part of the sentence,
we won’t capitalise it. These are cases where an em dash can easily replace a colon. For
example:

Jan needed a few more items to complete her job application: a résumé, cover letter, and
references.



Date
Acceptable formats
     ●​ DD Mmm YYYY (e.g. 22 Jan 2025)
     ●​ DD Month YYYY (e.g. 22 January 2025)
     ●​ DD/MM/YYYY (e.g. 22/01/2025) Note: since the American system switches the order
         of month and day, leading with this format is never a good idea and can cause
         confusion, especially when both the month and the day are below 13.
When it’s clear which year the date refers to, you can omit the year:
     ●​ DD Mmm (e.g. 22 Jan)
     ●​ DD Month (e.g. 22 January)
     ●​ DD/MM
It’s not necessary to keep the date format consistent within the same document. For
example, you can write:
         On Saturday, 22 Jan, there will be system maintenance for up to two hours. There
         might be another one on 25/02/2025.

What to avoid
Don't add “st”, “nd”, or “th” after the date; don’t write 1st Feb, 22nd Jan, 13th Mar.

Is it ok to...
Write the month in full form? Yes, if there is no character limit, e.g. when you're writing
emails, blog posts, and the like, and you feel the full form gives a more natural feeling and
sits organically in your sentence, go with it.



Time

Use the 12-hour/am/pm format. Use numerals followed by a space and am or pm. If the
hour is a single digit, use the H:MM format; if it's two digits, use HH:MM. Use an en dash
between times to indicate a time period.

As we’re writing for a global audience, use Greenwich Mean Time as the default time and
include its abbreviation in parentheses.

   ●​   9:00 am (GMT)
   ●​   9:30 am (GMT)
   ●​   9:30 am–10:30 am (GMT)
   ●​   11:30 am–1:00 pm (GMT)

Date and time
To combine date and time, use the following sequence:
    ●​ 3:20 pm (GMT) on Friday, 11 October 2019
    ●​ 3:20 pm (GMT) on 11 Oct 2019
    ●​ 3:20 pm (GMT), 11 Oct 2019
    ●​ 3:20 pm (GMT), Friday, 11 October 2019
Lists
    ●​   Introduce a list with a colon only if the lead-in statement is a complete sentence.
    ●​   Don’t use a punctuation mark if the lead-in statement is a sentence fragment.
    ●​   Start each list item with a capital letter.
    ●​   Don’t use a punctuation mark after the list items that are not complete sentences.
    ●​   Use normal terminal punctuation (period, question mark, or exclamation point) after
         the list items that are complete sentences (including imperative sentences).

Names and titles
Capitalise the names of departments and teams (but not the word “team” or “department”).
   ●​ Join the Marketing team...
   ●​ Our IT department…
   ●​ Our Front-end Development team…
   ●​ Customer Support team

Capitalise individual job titles if referencing a specific role, but don’t capitalise if referencing
in general terms.
    ●​ The new Marketing Director starts today.
    ●​ All the managers ate durian.

Capitalise academic degrees only when the full name of the degree is used, such as
Bachelor of Arts or Master of Social Work. General references, such as bachelor's, master's
or doctoral degree, are not capitalised. Do not capitalise graduate certificates. Names of
academic disciplines, majors, minors, programmes, and courses of study are not proper
nouns and should not be capitalised except for languages such as English, French, and
Japanese. Use an apostrophe with bachelor’s degree and master’s degree, but not in
Bachelor of Arts or Master of Science. Do not use an apostrophe with associate degree or
doctoral degree. Do not use periods in degree abbreviations.

    ●​ Bachelor of Arts in literature
    ●​ Bachelor of Science in biology
    ●​ She graduated with a bachelor’s degree in communication. (recommended for Deriv
       job descriptions)
    ●​ She’s planning to pursue a master’s degree in English. (recommended for Deriv job
       descriptions)
    ●​ She holds a doctoral degree (or doctorate) from Stanford University.

Currency
Use a specific three-letter currency code to indicate the currency you’re referring to.
   ●​ 1,000 USD
   ●​ 5 EUR
   ●​   5,344.00 GBP
   ●​   70 AUD
   ●​   USD account
   ●​   5.7 million USD
   ●​   2.5 BTC

When describing the currency, spell it out fully and don’t capitalise.
  ●​ You have successfully changed your currency to the euro.
  ●​ On the bright side, the pound sterling is not the worst-performing currency in the world
      today.
  ●​ The Australian dollar was hit hard overnight.
  ●​ GBP/USD is a currency pair that involves buying the pound sterling and selling the US
      dollar.
  ●​ Market intel suggests that investors should capitalise on the current price dip and buy
      bitcoin.

Don’t spell out the currency in full if describing an account type. Use the abbreviation or
three-letter currency code instead.
    ●​ You have added a BTC account.
    ●​ You do not have any balance remaining in your USDT account.
    ●​ You cannot change your account currency because you have deposited money into
       your USD account.

For cryptocurrencies, capitalise if you’re referring to the technology, concept, or units of the
currency.
    ●​ We still don’t know who created Bitcoin.
    ●​ Someone sent me 12 Bitcoins by mistake!
    ●​ If you want to trade with Litecoin as your account currency, switch to your LTC
       account.
    ●​ Trade cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, Ether, Ripple, and Litecoin without taking
       ownership of the underlying.

Numbers
Spell out a number when it begins a sentence unless it’s ridiculously long (whereby you
should rephrase the sentence so the number doesn’t come at the beginning). Otherwise, use
the numeral (including ordinals). An exception can be made for headlines if you’re starting
the sentence with a number.
    ●​ Three new developers will start on Monday, and 5 more next week.
    ●​ I bought 3 new pairs of shoes last weekend.
    ●​ We won 1st place in the Best Online Trading Provider category, an improvement over
       our 3rd place finish last year. ​
    ●​ 20 years of experience and trust (headline)
Use commas for numbers over three digits unless they’re part of a proper noun or used to
describe leverage.
   ●​ 345
   ●​ 1,200
   ●​ 4,637,900
   ●​ Leverage up to 1:2000
   ●​ Crash 1000 Index

Use the % symbol instead of spelling out “per cent.” It’s better to use numerals and not the
word for percentages, even for numbers less than 10, unless at the beginning of the
sentence (but try to rephrase so the number doesn’t come at the beginning if possible).
   ●​ Over 50% of our clients are from Brazil.
   ●​ Due to last week's pricing error, we had to refund 5% of our clients.
   ●​ Eighty per cent of retail traders lose their money.

When writing two numbers right next to each other, spell out the first number and use a
numeral for the second number to make it easier to read.
   ●​ We tested fifty-two 18-inch rims and found the Canary brand to be the best.
   ●​ We tested six 18-inch rims and found the Canary brand to be the best.

Hyperlinks
Avoid using “click here” which hurts usability, accessibility, and SEO. Links should clearly
explain where they will take you because you won’t have to remember the context if you
skim the page. Hyperlink the noun and not the verb phrase.
   ●​ For more information, visit the Help centre.

Abbreviations and acronyms
Spell out the meaning the first time, then use the shortened version after.
   ●​ You may trade contracts for difference (CFDs) on the Deriv MetaTrader 5 platform. CFD
       trading allows you to trade on a wide range of markets, from cryptocurrencies to
       metals, with up to 1:1000 leverage.
Commonly used terms

Log in vs login
Log in is a verb. For e.g. The system will direct you automatically after you log in.
Login can be a noun or an adjective. For e.g. Please keep your account login details safe.

Sign up vs sign-up
Sign up is a verb. For e.g. Sign up for an account now.
Sign-up can be a noun or an adjective. For e.g. We will redirect you to the sign-up page.
We do not use signup.

Deriv real account
Deriv real account, not real Deriv account.

Ebook
Ebook or ebook, not e-book, eBook, or e-Book.

Email
Email or email, not e-mail.

E-wallet
E-wallet or e-wallet, not ewallet.

Select vs selected
Using select as an adjective can cause a lot of confusion, so tread carefully.

The most common definition of select is (of a group of people or things) carefully chosen
from a larger number as being the best or most valuable.

The most common definition of selected is something that has been chosen or picked out
from a larger number.

Although certain sources allow for select to be used interchangeably with selected, it’s not
worth the hassle.

Among vs between
Among introduces more than two items, while between is only used when describing two
items. E.g. among us, between you and me.

Biometric vs biometrics
The word biometric is an adjective, meaning it describes a noun (e.g., biometric data,
biometric security). Biometrics is a noun that refers to the field of study, the technology, or
the unique physical/behavioral characteristics themselves.

Staff
Both singular and plural. Never use staffs unless you’re referring to the wooden kind.

Internet vs internet
It used to be common practice to capitalise references to the Internet. However, a number of
mainstream publications have moved to use internet in recent years as internet connectivity
has expanded. We use internet in all media.

Talent vs talents
Talent, when it means a talented person or talented people, remains talent, whether singular
or plural. Talents means natural abilities to do things well.

Zip code
Zip code is treated as a regular noun, not an acronym. Use Zip at the start of a sentence. Use
zip mid-sentence or in body copy.

   ●​ Zip code must be entered to continue.
   ●​ Enter your zip code to find local payment agents.

Avoid: ZIP code

   ●​ Be personal and engaging: Personalise messages and use compelling language to

Video scripts and voiceover guidelines

When crafting video scripts or voiceover content for Deriv, aim to maintain the same tone
and style as the rest of our communications — professional yet approachable, clear, and
informative. Since the audience is global, it’s essential to use jargon-free language and
simple sentences, ensuring that non-native English speakers can understand the message.

The scripts should be engaging and easy to follow, with content centred around the
audience’s needs and derived from our key tone of voice guidelines: clear and direct,
friendly and approachable, client-centred, trustworthy and transparent, informative
and engaging.
Clarity and simplicity
   ●​ Keep sentences short and straightforward to ensure quick comprehension.
   ●​ Use a conversational tone, avoiding over-complicated language or long sentences.
   ●​ Be mindful of a global audience – avoid idiomatic expressions or cultural references
        that may not translate well.

Hook the viewer early
   ●​ Start with a strong hook to capture the viewer's attention within the first 10 seconds.
   ●​ Highlight intriguing or key information early on to spark curiosity, and maintain
      engagement throughout the script.

Maintain natural flow
   ●​ Write as if speaking directly to the audience. Scripts for voiceovers should feel natural
      when spoken aloud.
   ●​ Use active voice to make the content more engaging and easier to digest.

Emphasise key messages
  ●​ Identify the three main takeaways for your audience and make sure these points are
     made clearly throughout the video.

End with a CTA
   ●​ Always finish with a call to action (CTA), whether signing up, visiting the website, or
      contacting customer support. The CTA should be concise, focused, and appropriate
      for the video’s objective.



Script formatting guidelines

Script sections
   ●​ Visual cues should be formatted in italics and enclosed in square brackets. Example:
       [Drone shot of office team interaction].
   ●​ Dialogue and voiceover text should be plain and follow natural speech patterns.
   ●​ Actions or specific notes should be enclosed in parentheses (for example, sound
       effect, pause, emphasis).

Instructions for voice talent
   ●​ Write emphasis words in bold to ensure special attention is given while delivering.
   ●​ Use pauses strategically – indicate where breaks or pauses for viewer
       comprehension would be effective.
